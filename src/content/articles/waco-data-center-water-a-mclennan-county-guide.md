## What is the Proposed Infrakey Data Center in McLennan County?

Infrakey, a major technology infrastructure developer, has proposed a massive $10 billion data center complex in unincorporated McLennan County. ## Why Are McLennan County Residents Concerned About Water?

The primary concern among McLennan County residents centers on the immense volume of water a data center of this size could require. Data centers use water primarily for cooling their extensive server banks, which generate considerable heat. The reported water demand for the Infrakey project is approximately 2 million gallons per day (GPD). To put this into perspective, 2 million gallons is roughly enough water to supply nearly 20,000 average Texas households daily.

Residents are rightly worried about the impact such a demand could have on local water sources, especially in a region that has faced drought conditions and relies on both surface water and the Trinity Aquifer. Local opposition to the project has been significant, with a petition reportedly gathering around 3,000 signatures and a dedicated Facebook group forming to organize resistance. State Representative Pat Curry has also become involved, reflecting the community's deep concern over the project's potential environmental footprint.

## Water Rights and Responsibilities: Who Supplies the Infrakey Project?

The situation regarding water supply for the Infrakey project is complex due to its location in unincorporated McLennan County but within Waco's water service area. In Texas, surface water rights are generally allocated by the state, while groundwater is subject to the "rule of capture," allowing landowners to pump water from beneath their property with few limits, though groundwater conservation districts can impose regulations.

If the data center intends to connect to a municipal water supply, such as Waco's, it would need to negotiate an agreement with the city. As of recent reports, Waco had not been approached for such an agreement, despite the significant stated water demand. This raises questions about how the project intends to secure its water. Potential options could include:

*   **Connecting to Waco's municipal supply:** This would require a formal agreement and could place significant strain on the city's existing infrastructure and water rights.
*   **Drilling private wells:** If the land on which the data center is located has sufficient groundwater, Infrakey could potentially drill its own wells, subject to any local groundwater conservation district regulations. This would directly impact the aquifer and potentially nearby private wells.
*   **Other regional water providers:** The project could seek water from another utility or water district, if available and economically feasible.

Landowners near a data center that drills its own wells should be particularly vigilant. A sudden increase in pumping can lower the water table, causing private wells to run dry or requiring costly well deepening. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can McLennan County Pause or Stop the Infrakey Project?
McLennan County's authority over a project in unincorporated areas is generally limited to zoning (if a county zoning ordinance exists, which is rare for general counties in Texas) and specific permitting related to roads, drainage, or septic systems. However, counties can exert influence through public pressure, coordination with state agencies, and by denying permits within their direct authority. Strong community opposition can significantly impact a project's viability and lead to additional scrutiny from state regulators and elected officials.

### Does Waco Have to Supply Water to a Project in its Service Area?
While the Infrakey project is located within Waco's water service area, the city is not automatically obligated to supply water without a formal agreement. Such an agreement would typically involve negotiations over supply volume, cost, infrastructure upgrades, and potential impact fees. Waco, like any municipal utility, must consider the needs of its existing customers and the long-term sustainability of its water resources when evaluating new large-scale connections.

### What Legal Rights Do Landowners Have if a Data Center Impacts Their Water Supply?
In Texas, if a data center's operations, particularly groundwater pumping, cause direct harm to your property, such as your well running dry or experiencing significant reduction in yield, you may have legal recourse. This often involves demonstrating a direct causal link between the data center's actions and your specific harm. Documenting your well's baseline conditions and any changes is critical evidence. This is general information, not legal advice for your specific situation.

### How Can I Monitor Local Water Levels and Quality?
You can monitor local groundwater levels through the Texas Water Development Board (TWDB) observation well network, which provides data on aquifer levels across the state. For your own well, regular measurements of water depth and periodic water quality testing can help establish a baseline and detect any changes. Private labs offer water quality testing, which can identify contaminants or changes in mineral content.
