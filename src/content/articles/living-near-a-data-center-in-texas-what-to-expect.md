Living near a data center in Texas usually means living next to a large, industrial building that runs 24 hours a day — and the day-to-day realities are constant fan noise, periodic diesel-generator exhaust, bright overnight lighting, and heavy demand on local water and power. None of that means your life becomes unbearable, and many neighbors coexist with these facilities. But knowing what to expect, and what to do about it, is how you protect your home and your investment before problems set in.

Big Data Damage exists to help Texas landowners on exactly this point. We are not against data centers or the technology they run. We believe that when a facility's impacts land on the people next door, those people deserve transparency and fair treatment. Here is a practical, honest picture of what living near one is like — and the steps that matter.

## What is it like to live near a data center?

Expect a large, always-on industrial neighbor rather than a dramatic disaster. A data center is typically a windowless building — or a cluster of them — surrounded by security fencing, transformers, cooling equipment, and generators. It operates continuously, so the effects are less about isolated events and more about **constant, low-grade change** to a once-quiet setting: a persistent hum, lights that never go dark, more truck traffic during construction and maintenance, and new power lines crossing the landscape.

The intensity depends heavily on the facility's design and how far your property sits from it. A well-set-back campus with modern, quieter cooling affects neighbors differently than an older evaporative-cooled facility close to the property line. The four sections below cover the impacts neighbors report most.

## Will the noise from a data center affect my home and sleep?

It can, because the cooling systems that keep servers from overheating run continuously and produce a steady, low-frequency hum. Unlike traffic or a passing train, the sound does not stop, and low-frequency noise carries well across open rural land and can be noticeable inside a home at night — which is why sleep disruption is one of the most common complaints from data center neighbors.

Texas does not regulate this noise through a single state permit; instead, persistent noise that substantially interferes with the use and enjoyment of your property is addressed through **nuisance law** and, in some places, local ordinances. If you can hear a facility inside your home with the windows closed, that is worth documenting carefully. Our guide to [Texas data center noise and nuisance law](/articles/data-center-noise-texas-nuisance-law) explains how these claims work.

## Is the air around a data center safe?

The main air concern is the **diesel backup generators** that many data centers install to keep running during power outages. These generators are tested regularly and can run during grid strain, releasing exhaust — including fine particulates and other pollutants — that nearby residents breathe. Between tests and outages they are idle, so exposure is intermittent rather than constant, but for close neighbors it is a legitimate concern.

These emissions are regulated by the **Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ)**, which issues air permits and takes public comment before approving them. If a facility near you is seeking or modifying an air permit, you have the right to participate in that process. Our [air damage hub](/damage/air) covers what those permits do and how to weigh in.

## Will living near a data center lower my property value?

It might, depending on your specific property and the facility — there is no automatic, fixed amount. The factors that can reduce value are the same ones that make a home less pleasant to live in: industrial views, all-night lighting, constant noise, altered drainage, and increased traffic. A home that is harder to enjoy is generally harder to sell for the same price.

We are careful not to overstate this. There is no blanket rule that a data center drops every neighbor's value by a set percentage, and we do not claim one. Where a facility does cause a permanent, substantial interference with your property, Texas nuisance law may measure the harm as lost market value — but that turns on the facts. ## What can I do to protect myself and my property?

The single most valuable thing you can do is **build a record now**, before any harm occurs, so you are not trying to reconstruct conditions after the fact. Concrete steps:

1. **Document a baseline.** Photograph and video your property, note your well's performance and water quality, and consider a professional appraisal before construction. A dated "before" snapshot is the foundation of any future claim.
2. **Keep an impacts log.** If the facility is built, record specific problems with dates and times — "hum audible in bedroom from 11 p.m.," "generator exhaust visible on test days," "standing water in the pasture after rain."
3. **Watch the permits and meetings.** Follow TCEQ air-permit notices and any groundwater district proceedings, and attend the city or county meetings where the project is discussed. These are your formal chances to be heard.
4. **Preserve communications.** Save correspondence with the developer and local officials, and summarize phone calls in writing.
5. **Know your water position.** If your household relies on a well, understand your rights and options — see [what to do if your well runs dry](/articles/what-to-do-if-your-well-runs-dry) and the statewide data from the [Texas Water Development Board](https://www.twdb.texas.gov/).

## What this means legally for a Texas landowner

Your strongest protections as a neighbor are **nuisance law and your property and water rights**, but each depends on documented facts. Nuisance law protects against conditions — noise, light, fumes — that substantially interfere with the use and enjoyment of your land, and a permanent nuisance can in some cases be valued as lost market value. Your groundwater is recognized as a property right in Texas. And where public infrastructure like transmission lines crosses or takes your land, the Texas Constitution requires adequate compensation.

This is general information, not legal advice, and no outcome is guaranteed. Whether you have a claim depends entirely on your facts — which is why the record you build today is worth more than any prediction anyone can make now.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How close is too close to a data center?

There is no fixed legal setback that applies statewide; impact depends on the facility's size, cooling method, generator placement, and lighting, and on your property's position relative to it. The closer you are and the fewer natural buffers between you and the facility, the more likely you are to notice noise, light, and exhaust. Documenting conditions at your specific distance is what matters.

### Can I do anything about the constant noise?

Yes — persistent noise that substantially interferes with the enjoyment of your property may be addressed under Texas nuisance law and, in some areas, local ordinances. The key is documentation: dated recordings, a log of when the noise is worst, and notes on where in your home it is audible. That record is what supports any complaint or claim.

### Are the diesel generators dangerous to my health?

The backup generators emit exhaust when tested or running during outages, which is a real concern for close neighbors, though exposure is intermittent rather than continuous. Their emissions are permitted and regulated by the TCEQ, and you can participate in the air-permit process. If you have health concerns, document them and the facility's operating patterns.

### Should I sell before a data center is built nearby?

That is a personal financial decision, not one to make on assumption. Because a data center's effect on value is specific rather than automatic, the informed move is to get an independent appraisal and understand the actual proposed footprint before deciding. Selling in a panic can cost you as much as staying uninformed.

### Where can I find official information about a nearby project?

Air permits come through the [TCEQ](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/), water and groundwater matters run through your local groundwater conservation district, and land-use decisions happen in city council and county commissioners' court meetings — all of which take public input. Statewide water data is published by the Texas Water Development Board.

## The bottom line for data center neighbors

Living near a data center in Texas means adjusting to an always-on industrial neighbor — most often noise, light, intermittent exhaust, and pressure on shared water. It is manageable, and it is not a catastrophe, but it is also not nothing, and you do not have to simply absorb whatever comes. By documenting your baseline, watching the permits, and understanding your rights, you put yourself in a position to protect your home and to insist on fair treatment if the facility's impacts reach your property.
