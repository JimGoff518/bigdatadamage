Data centers are not inherently bad — but they can be very bad for the specific people who live next to them if the harm they cause is never accounted for. That is the honest answer to a question a lot of Texas landowners are typing lately. These buildings power the internet and the AI tools millions of people rely on. The problem is not that they exist. The problem is when a billion-dollar facility draws down a rural community's water, fouls its air, and runs around the clock next to homes — and no one is made whole for it.

Big Data Damage takes that distinction seriously. We are not an anti-technology project. We believe the people who bear the real costs of data center development — in water, air, quiet, and property value — deserve transparency and fair compensation. This is an honest look at where the genuine harms are, and where they are not.

## Are data centers bad for nearby Texas landowners?

They can be, when their impacts land on neighbors who get none of the benefit. A data center brings tax revenue and a handful of long-term jobs to a region, but the burdens — water demand, diesel exhaust, constant noise, heavy construction, and new transmission lines — fall hardest on the closest properties. When those burdens are disclosed, mitigated, and fairly compensated, a data center can be a manageable neighbor. When they are hidden or ignored, the nearby landowner absorbs a loss they never agreed to.

The rest of this article walks through the four harms that come up most often, so you can judge a specific project on its actual footprint rather than on hype in either direction.

## How much water do data centers use?

Large data centers can use a lot of water — often hundreds of thousands to millions of gallons a day at full operation — because many cool their servers with evaporative systems that consume water continuously. In water-stressed parts of Texas, that demand competes directly with farms, ranches, and household wells for a limited supply.

The amount varies enormously by design. Some newer facilities use closed-loop or air cooling that consumes far less water; others rely on evaporative cooling that consumes a great deal. That is why the single most important question to ask about any project is a specific one: how many gallons per day, from what source, and what happens to the local supply if that source falls short. Statewide water planning data is published by the [Texas Water Development Board](https://www.twdb.texas.gov/), and we cover the water issue in depth on our [water damage hub](/damage/water).

## Do data centers cause noise and air pollution?

Yes — the two most common day-to-day complaints from data center neighbors are constant noise and diesel exhaust. The cooling fans and equipment at a large facility run 24 hours a day, producing a persistent low hum that can carry across quiet rural properties and interfere with sleep. Many facilities also install banks of **diesel backup generators**, which are tested regularly and can run during grid strain, releasing exhaust that neighbors breathe.

Air emissions from those generators are regulated by the **Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ)**, which issues and enforces air permits — a process the public can participate in. Noise is generally addressed through Texas nuisance law rather than a state permit. We break both down on our [air damage hub](/damage/air) and in our guide to [Texas data center noise and nuisance law](/articles/data-center-noise-texas-nuisance-law).

## Can a data center lower my property value?

It can, though whether it does depends on the property and the project. Industrial-scale buildings, security lighting that burns all night, altered drainage, heavy traffic, and round-the-clock noise can all make a nearby home less desirable — and a less desirable home is worth less on the open market. A permanent, substantial interference with the use and enjoyment of your land can, under Texas nuisance law, be measured in the lost market value of the property.

It is important to be accurate here: there is no blanket rule that living near a data center automatically drops every property's value by a fixed amount, and we do not claim one. The effect is specific to each situation, which is exactly why documenting your property's condition and value matters. ## What rights do Texas landowners have?

Texas landowners have real, if specific, legal protections — centered on water rights, nuisance, and just compensation. Texas follows the "rule of capture" for groundwater, and a landowner's water beneath their land is recognized as a property right, so pumping that damages a neighbor's supply can raise legal questions. Nuisance law protects against conditions that substantially interfere with the use and enjoyment of property, including noise, light, and fumes. And the Texas Constitution guarantees "adequate compensation" when property is taken or damaged for a public use, such as the transmission lines and pipelines that support these facilities.

None of these rights is automatic, and none of this is legal advice. Whether a claim exists depends on the facts — what is built, how it operates, and how it affects a particular property. The practical first step for any concerned landowner is to build a record: document your water, your property's condition, and any impacts, with dates.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are data centers bad for the environment?

They can strain local resources — especially water and the electric grid — and their diesel backup generators emit air pollutants. But impact varies widely by design and location; a facility using air or closed-loop cooling in a water-rich area is very different from an evaporative-cooled facility in a drought-stressed region. The honest measure is the specific project's water source, cooling method, and emissions, not data centers as a category.

### Are data centers bad for people who live near them?

For the closest neighbors, the most common problems are constant fan noise, diesel exhaust from backup generators, night lighting, and pressure on shared water. Whether those rise to the level of legal harm depends on the facility and the property, but they are the real, documented complaints — not hypothetical ones.

### Is Big Data Damage against data centers?

No. We are not anti-technology and not anti-AI. Our position is that landowners who are harmed by data center development — through water loss, air pollution, noise, or diminished property value — deserve transparency and fair compensation. Good projects that disclose and mitigate their impacts are not the concern.

### What should I do if a data center is proposed near me?

Document your property's baseline now — water levels, well performance, photographs, and dates — and stay informed through local public meetings and any permit filings. Watch for TCEQ air permits and groundwater district proceedings, both of which allow public participation. Keeping a written record protects your position if harm later occurs.

## The honest bottom line

Are data centers bad? Not as a category — but they can impose very real, very local harms, and those harms are too often left uncompensated. The right response is neither to wave the projects through nor to oppose all of them on principle. It is to demand straight answers about water, air, noise, and property impact, and to make sure the landowners who bear the cost are treated fairly. That is the standard every project should have to meet.
